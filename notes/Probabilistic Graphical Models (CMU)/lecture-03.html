Probabilistic Graphical Models

Chapter 3: Variable Elimination

Notes by Mihir Rao

The previous two chapters were about representation: how to compactly describe a joint distribution using a graph. This chapter is about inference: how to answer probabilistic questions from that representation. Given a Bayesian network or Markov random field, we will repeatedly want to compute things like $\mathbb{P}(X_i = x)$, $\mathbb{P}(X_i \mid \text{evidence})$, or $\arg\max_x \mathbb{P}(x)$. In general these problems are NP-hard — but exploiting the graph structure with a dynamic-programming algorithm called variable elimination makes them tractable for many models of interest.

3.1  The Two Inference Problems

Two kinds of question come up constantly.

Definition [marginal inference]. Given a joint distribution $p(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ and a subset $Y \subseteq \{X_1, \ldots, X_n\}$ of query variables, compute the marginal $$p(y) \;=\; \sum_{x_1, \ldots, x_n \setminus y} p(x_1, \ldots, x_n).$$
Definition [MAP inference]. Given $p$, find the most likely joint assignment: $$x^\star \;=\; \arg\max_{x_1, \ldots, x_n}\, p(x_1, \ldots, x_n).$$

Both problems are NP-hard in general — there are joint distributions for which no algorithm can be polynomial in $n$ unless P = NP. But the practical situation is much friendlier: when the graphical structure is sparse enough, both problems can be solved in time polynomial in the size of the model. Variable elimination is the basic exact-inference algorithm that exploits the graph structure to do so.

3.2  A Motivating Example: Inference on a Chain

The clearest illustration is the chain Bayesian network $$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \;=\; p(x_1) \prod_{i=2}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_{i-1}),$$ where each variable takes values in a domain of size $k$. Suppose we want the marginal $p(x_n)$.

The naive way. By definition, $$p(x_n) \;=\; \sum_{x_1} \sum_{x_2} \cdots \sum_{x_{n-1}} p(x_1) \prod_{i=2}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_{i-1}).$$ There are $k^{n-1}$ assignments to sum over, and each term costs $O(n)$ multiplications, giving total time $\mathcal{O}(n k^n)$. For $n = 30$ binary variables this is already astronomical.

The dynamic-programming way. Notice that each conditional $p(x_i \mid x_{i-1})$ depends only on the two adjacent variables. We can push the summations inward to the smallest scope they affect: $$p(x_n) \;=\; \sum_{x_{n-1}} p(x_n \mid x_{n-1}) \sum_{x_{n-2}} p(x_{n-1} \mid x_{n-2}) \cdots \sum_{x_1} p(x_2 \mid x_1)\,p(x_1).$$ The innermost sum $\sum_{x_1} p(x_2 \mid x_1)\,p(x_1)$ is a function of $x_2$ alone — call it $\tau_2(x_2)$ — and computing it costs $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$: for each of $k$ values of $x_2$, we sum over $k$ values of $x_1$. With $\tau_2$ in hand, the next sum becomes $\tau_3(x_3) = \sum_{x_2} p(x_3 \mid x_2)\,\tau_2(x_2)$, again $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$, and so on.

Each elimination step is $\mathcal{O}(k^2)$, and we perform $n - 1$ of them, so the total cost is $\mathcal{O}(n k^2)$ — linear in the chain length. The same answer, exponentially faster. The trick that bought this is pure dynamic programming: identify subproblems whose answers can be reused, store them, and propagate.

3.3  Factor Operations

To generalise from chains to arbitrary graphs, we need to fix vocabulary. A graphical model factorises as a product of factors: $$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z}\, \prod_{c \in C} \phi_c(x_c).$$ For a Bayesian network, the factors are CPDs and $Z = 1$; for an MRF, they are clique potentials and $Z$ is the partition function. The variable-elimination algorithm manipulates factors using two basic operations: product and marginalisation.

Definition [factor product]. Given two factors $\phi_1(X^{(1)})$ and $\phi_2(X^{(2)})$, their product is a factor whose scope is $X^{(1)} \cup X^{(2)}$, defined for each joint assignment $x$ by $$\phi_3(x_{1 \cup 2}) \;=\; \phi_1(x_{(1)}) \cdot \phi_2(x_{(2)}).$$

If $\phi_1$ has scope $\{A, B\}$ and $\phi_2$ has scope $\{B, C\}$, then $\phi_1 \cdot \phi_2$ has scope $\{A, B, C\}$, and for each $(a, b, c)$ we take the product of the two factor entries that match those values. The cost of building the product is proportional to the size of the result table — $\mathcal{O}(k^3)$ in this case.

Definition [factor marginalisation]. Given a factor $\phi(X, Y)$ with scope partitioned into two subsets $X$ and $Y$, marginalising out $Y$ produces a factor on $X$: $$\tau(x) \;=\; \sum_{y} \phi(x, y).$$

Marginalisation does not generally produce a probability distribution — $\tau$ is just a non-negative function over $X$. If the original $\phi$ was already a properly normalised joint, then $\tau$ is the corresponding marginal; in the middle of an elimination pass, $\tau$ is just an intermediate quantity that will be multiplied with other factors before any final normalisation.

3.4  The Variable Elimination Algorithm

With those operations in place, the algorithm is short.

Algorithm — Variable Elimination
# input: factor set $\Phi = \{\phi_c\}$, elimination ordering $O$ over variables to remove, query $Y$
# output: a factor proportional to $p(Y)$
for each $X_i$ in $O$:
  1. Collect $\Phi_i \subseteq \Phi$ — the factors whose scope contains $X_i$.
  2. Multiply them: $\sigma_i \leftarrow \prod_{\phi \in \Phi_i} \phi$.
  3. Marginalise: $\tau_i(x) \leftarrow \sum_{X_i} \sigma_i(x, X_i)$.
  4. Replace: $\Phi \leftarrow (\Phi \setminus \Phi_i) \cup \{\tau_i\}$.
return $\prod_{\phi \in \Phi} \phi$

Each iteration sums one variable out and replaces the factors that mentioned it with a single intermediate factor over their residual scopes. The final product, after every elimination has happened, is proportional to the marginal $p(Y)$; one final normalisation divides by its total mass if a probability is desired.

3.5  A Worked Example: The Student Grades Model

Take the student-grades Bayesian network from chapter 1, with variables difficulty $d$, intelligence $i$, grade $g$, SAT score $s$, and letter quality $l$, and joint $$p(l, g, i, d, s) \;=\; p(l \mid g)\, p(g \mid i, d)\, p(i)\, p(d)\, p(s \mid i).$$ Suppose we want the marginal $p(l)$ using the elimination ordering $d, i, s, g$.

Eliminate $d$. The factors mentioning $d$ are $p(g \mid i, d)$ and $p(d)$. Multiply and sum: $$\tau_1(g, i) \;=\; \sum_d p(g \mid i, d)\, p(d).$$ The factor $p(d)$ disappears from the factor set, $p(g \mid i, d)$ is replaced by $\tau_1(g, i)$, and the remaining set is $\{p(l \mid g),\, \tau_1(g, i),\, p(i),\, p(s \mid i)\}$.

Eliminate $i$. The factors mentioning $i$ are $\tau_1(g, i)$, $p(i)$, and $p(s \mid i)$. Multiply and sum: $$\tau_2(g, s) \;=\; \sum_i \tau_1(g, i)\, p(i)\, p(s \mid i).$$ The set becomes $\{p(l \mid g),\, \tau_2(g, s)\}$.

Eliminate $s$. Only $\tau_2(g, s)$ mentions $s$: $$\tau_3(g) \;=\; \sum_s \tau_2(g, s).$$ The set becomes $\{p(l \mid g),\, \tau_3(g)\}$.

Eliminate $g$. Both remaining factors mention $g$: $$p(l) \;=\; \sum_g p(l \mid g)\, \tau_3(g).$$ The result is the desired marginal $p(l)$.

Each intermediate factor has scope at most two variables, so every step's cost is $\mathcal{O}(k^3)$ (multiplying two binary factors gives a ternary factor, which we then sum out a variable from). Over four eliminations the total cost is also $\mathcal{O}(k^3)$ — far cheaper than the naive $\mathcal{O}(k^5)$ marginalisation of a five-variable joint.

3.6  Incorporating Evidence

Marginal queries are usually conditional: we want $p(Y \mid E = e)$ for some observed evidence $E = e$ rather than the raw marginal $p(Y)$. By Bayes' rule $$p(Y \mid E = e) \;=\; \frac{p(Y, E = e)}{p(E = e)},$$ both numerator and denominator are themselves marginal queries, each solvable by variable elimination.

The standard trick is to reduce factors with evidence at the start: replace every factor $\phi(X)$ whose scope contains an evidence variable with the slice obtained by fixing that variable to its observed value. The reduced factor has lower-dimensional scope and the same numerical entries. Then run variable elimination on the residual factor set with the evidence variables removed from the elimination ordering. The output is a factor over $Y$ proportional to the joint $p(Y, E = e)$; renormalising gives the conditional $p(Y \mid E = e)$.

3.7  Complexity

The cost of a single elimination step is dominated by the size of the intermediate factor it produces, which is exponential in that factor's scope. Let $M$ be the largest scope size of any intermediate factor created during the elimination pass. Then the total running time is $$\mathcal{O}\bigl( n \cdot k^{M+1} \bigr),$$ where $n$ is the number of variables and $k$ is the maximum domain size of any variable. The exponent is determined by $M$, not by $n$ — so the algorithm is polynomial in $n$ for any fixed $M$, but the constant $M$ depends crucially on the elimination ordering.

Example [ordering matters]. In the student-grades example above, eliminating in the order $d, i, s, g$ kept every intermediate factor's scope at most two, so $M = 2$. Had we eliminated $g$ first, the factors mentioning it — $p(l \mid g)$ and $p(g \mid i, d)$ — would have multiplied to a factor with scope $\{l, i, d\}$, immediately pushing $M$ to 3. On larger graphs the wrong ordering can blow up the intermediate scopes to size $n$, recovering the original exponential cost.

3.8  Choosing an Elimination Ordering

Finding the ordering that minimises $M$ is NP-hard in general — itself an instance of a known graph problem (computing the treewidth of the graph). In practice we rely on greedy heuristics that pick the next variable to eliminate based on a local criterion:

  • Min-neighbours — pick the variable currently sharing a factor with the fewest other variables.
  • Min-weight — pick the variable that, when eliminated, would produce the smallest intermediate factor measured by the product of its variables' domain sizes.
  • Min-fill — pick the variable whose elimination would add the fewest new "fill" edges between its neighbours.

None of these is guaranteed to find an optimal ordering, but all are cheap to compute and tend to perform well on real models. The underlying graph-theoretic notion they target is the treewidth: for tree-structured Bayesian networks the treewidth is $1$, and variable elimination runs in time linear in $n$. As the graph becomes denser, the treewidth grows, and exact inference becomes correspondingly more expensive — eventually intractable, at which point approximate inference (the subject of later chapters) becomes the practical option.

3.9  Summary

  • Inference in graphical models splits into two basic problems: marginal queries $p(Y)$ and MAP queries $\arg\max_x p(x)$. Both are NP-hard in general but tractable when the graph is sparse enough.
  • Variable elimination is the basic exact-inference algorithm. It pushes summations inward along the factorisation, building intermediate factors via the two operations of factor product and factor marginalisation.
  • On a chain of $n$ variables with domain size $k$, the algorithm runs in $\mathcal{O}(n k^2)$, an exponential speedup over the naive $\mathcal{O}(n k^n)$ marginalisation.
  • Evidence is incorporated by reducing factors to their slices at observed values, then running variable elimination on the residual factor set.
  • The total cost is $\mathcal{O}(n k^{M+1})$ where $M$ is the largest intermediate scope, which depends on the elimination ordering. Choosing the optimal ordering is NP-hard; the standard heuristics are min-neighbours, min-weight, and min-fill.
  • The underlying graph parameter is treewidth: trees have treewidth $1$ and inference is linear; dense graphs have high treewidth and force us toward approximate methods.
