Probabilistic Graphical Models

Chapter 3: Directed Graphical Models: Bayesian Networks

Notes by Mihir Rao

Chapter 2 built the undirected graphical model: a graph plus clique potentials, with edges that encoded coupling between variables and Markov properties read off by graph separation. We now turn to the directed analogue. The edges in a Bayesian network carry an arrow that encodes a direct influence of one variable on another — often a causal or generative reading — and the joint distribution factorises along the graph as a product of conditionals "node given its parents". The two model classes overlap but neither contains the other.

3.1  Two Types of Graphical Models

Directed edges give a causal or generative reading. A directed graphical model — a Bayesian network (BN) — factors as a product of conditional probabilities of each node given its parents:

$$P(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \;=\; \prod_{i = 1}^{n} P(X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}),$$

where $\mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}$ is the set of parents of $X_i$. Undirected edges, by contrast, give only correlations. The Markov random field of Chapter 2 factors as a Gibbs distribution over cliques, with a partition function for normalisation. Both can describe the same joint, but their structural assumptions are different, and the right choice depends on whether direction in the graph means something to the modeller.

3.2  What Is a Bayesian Network?

Definition. Bayesian network A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes represent random variables and whose edges represent direct influence of one variable on another. The graph provides a skeleton for representing a joint distribution in factored form, and a compact encoding of a set of conditional-independence assumptions about that distribution.

The picture to keep in mind is a generative sampling process executed by nature. Pick a topological order of the nodes; for each node, draw its value from a distribution that depends only on its parents. Sweep through the topological order and the joint $P(\mathbf{X})$ is fully sampled. Each variable is a stochastic function of its parents and nothing else — the graph encodes a strong locality assumption that is what makes the model tractable.

This causal-process reading is intuitive but not load-bearing. Bayesian networks can be used purely for compact representation of conditional independencies, with no claim about causation; the graph still factorises the joint correctly, and inference and learning still go through. We will be careful in later chapters to separate "directed edges mean causation" from "directed edges mean factorisation".

3.3  The Factorisation Theorem

Theorem (factorisation). Given a DAG $G$ on variables $X_1, \ldots, X_d$, the most general probability distribution consistent with the graph factors according to "node given its parents": $$P(\mathbf{X}) \;=\; \prod_{i = 1}^{d} P(X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}),$$ where $\mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}$ is the set of parents of $X_i$ in $G$.

For the cellular DAG above, the factorisation is a product of eight terms, $\mathbb{P}(X_1)\mathbb{P}(X_2)\mathbb{P}(X_3 \mid X_1)\,\cdots\,\mathbb{P}(X_8 \mid X_5, X_6)$. The general parameter count drops from $k^d - 1$ for the joint table to $\sum_i k^{|\pi_i| + 1}$ for the conditional tables, an exponential saving whenever the graph is sparse.

3.4  Three Local Structures

Three primitive three-node configurations exhaust the vocabulary of conditional independence in a DAG. Every more complex pattern can be read as a composition of these.

The asymmetry of the V-structure is the one fact every directed-graph student needs to know. Burglars and earthquakes are independent until you observe an alarm in your house; then learning that there was an earthquake "explains away" the alarm and lowers the posterior probability of a burglary. The configuration $A \to C \leftarrow B$ encodes this. The other two configurations behave the way undirected separation behaves — conditioning on the middle node blocks.

The common-parent claim is a one-line calculation. With $B$ a parent of both $A$ and $C$, the BN factorisation gives

$$P(A, C \mid B) \;=\; \frac{P(A, B, C)}{P(B)} \;=\; \frac{P(B)\,P(A \mid B)\,P(C \mid B)}{P(B)} \;=\; P(A \mid B)\,P(C \mid B),$$

so $A \perp\!\!\!\perp C \mid B$. The cascade is identical after substituting $P(A) P(B \mid A) P(C \mid B)$ for the joint. The V-structure does not give the same algebra, and the dependence after conditioning is real.

3.5  Local Markov Assumptions

Generalising from "three nodes" to "any DAG" gives the local Markov property.

Definition. Local Markov property Let $\mathrm{Pa}_{X_i}$ denote the parents of $X_i$ in $G$ and $\mathrm{NonDescendants}_{X_i}$ the variables that are not descendants of $X_i$. Then $G$ encodes the conditional-independence set $$I_\ell(G) \;=\; \bigl\{\, X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathrm{NonDescendants}_{X_i} \,\bigm|\, \mathrm{Pa}_{X_i} \,\bigr\}_{\forall\, i}.$$ In words: each node is independent of its non-descendants given its parents.

3.6  D-Separation: the Global Markov Property

The three local structures combine to give a global criterion for reading off conditional independence from the whole graph at once: d-separation.

Definition. d-separation Variables $X$ and $Y$ are d-separated by $Z$ in $G$, written $\mathrm{dsep}_G(X; Y \mid Z)$, if every path from $X$ to $Y$ is blocked by $Z$. A path is blocked at an interior node $W$ when either of the following holds:
  • $W$ is a cascade ($\to W \to$) or a common parent ($\leftarrow W \rightarrow$) on the path, and $W \in Z$;
  • $W$ is a V-structure ($\to W \leftarrow$) on the path, and neither $W$ nor any descendant of $W$ is in $Z$.
When every path is blocked, $X$ and $Y$ are d-separated. The induced independence set is $$I(G) \;=\; \bigl\{\,X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z \;:\; \mathrm{dsep}_G(X; Y \mid Z)\,\bigr\}.$$

An equivalent procedural test is the Bayes-ball algorithm: drop a ball at $X$ and let it bounce through the graph following rules that mirror the three local structures. If it can reach $Y$, the path is not blocked. A second equivalent test is to build the moralised ancestral graph — restrict to ancestors of $\{X, Y, Z\}$, add an undirected edge between every pair of parents of a common child, drop directions — and ask whether $Z$ separates $X$ from $Y$ in the resulting undirected graph in the sense of Chapter 2. The two views are interchangeable.

3.7  The Equivalence Theorem

The two specifications of a Bayesian network — the factorisation and the conditional-independence set $I(G)$ — give the same family of distributions, the directed analogue of Hammersley–Clifford.

Theorem (equivalence). Fix a DAG $G$, and let $\mathcal{D}_1$ denote the family of distributions that satisfy $I(G)$ and $\mathcal{D}_2$ the family that factor according to $G$. Then $\mathcal{D}_1 \equiv \mathcal{D}_2$.

For the graph to be useful, any conditional-independence we can derive from the structure must hold in every distribution the graph represents — that is the soundness direction. And every factored distribution must satisfy those independencies — that is completeness on the structural side. The theorem packages both into a single equivalence.

3.8  CPDs: Conditional Distributions at Each Node

Once the graph is fixed, what remains is to choose the conditional probability distribution (CPD) at each node — the local function $P(X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i})$. For discrete variables this is a conditional probability table (CPT), one row per joint setting of the parents. For continuous variables it is a parameterised density: a typical example is the linear Gaussian, $X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\beta_0 + \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i},\,\sigma_i^2\bigr)$. Any choice of CPD form is fine so long as the graph is honoured: the parents of $X_i$ are exactly the conditioning set.

Definition. Bayesian network (full) A Bayesian network is a pair $(G, P)$ where $P$ factorises over $G$ and $P$ is specified as the set of CPDs associated with the nodes of $G$.

3.9  Soundness and Completeness

Two formal questions about d-separation and the BN factorisation parallel those for the undirected case.

Theorem (soundness). If a distribution $P$ factorises according to $G$, then $I(G) \subseteq I(P)$.

Any independence the graph derives really holds in any distribution that factors over the graph. The converse — completeness in the strict sense — would say that if $X$ and $Y$ are not d-separated given $Z$, then $X$ and $Y$ are dependent in every distribution that factors over $G$. That stronger claim is false: a particular parameterisation can introduce extra independencies the graph never asserted. For example, $G: A \to B$ paired with $P$ such that $P(B \mid A = 0) = P(B \mid A = 1)$ factors over $G$ but has $A \perp\!\!\!\perp B$, an independence the graph does not encode. The weaker, "almost-everywhere" version is what holds.

Theorem (completeness, weak form). If $X$ and $Y$ are not d-separated given $Z$ in $G$, then $X$ and $Y$ are dependent in some distribution $P$ that factorises over $G$. Equivalently, for all $P$ that factorise over $G$ except for a set of measure zero in the space of CPD parameterisations, $I(P) = I(G)$.

3.10  I-Equivalence

Different DAGs can encode the same conditional-independence set, in which case any distribution that factors over one factors over the other. They are then said to be I-equivalent.

Definition. I-equivalence Two BN graphs $G_1$ and $G_2$ over the same variables are I-equivalent if $I(G_1) = I(G_2)$. The set of all DAGs partitions into mutually exclusive equivalence classes under this relation.

The practical consequence: an algorithm that learns BN structure from independence tests cannot in general determine the direction of an arrow on a chain or fork — only V-structures pin direction down. Two I-equivalent graphs are observationally indistinguishable, and choosing between them needs an additional assumption (typically a causal one).

3.11  Plate Notation

Many Bayesian models repeat the same structural pattern across $n$ conditionally independent observations. Drawing the pattern $n$ times is wasteful; plate notation encloses the repeated portion in a rectangle (a "plate") labelled with the range of the index. Variables inside the plate are replicated independently for each value of the index.

3.12  Special Case: Hidden Markov Models

A natural way to introduce time into a model with conditionally independent observations is to let the latent variable itself evolve. Promoting the single latent $\theta$ of a mixture model to a sequence $Y_1, \ldots, Y_T$ with first-order Markov dynamics yields the hidden Markov model (HMM): a "dynamic mixture" in which the cluster index walks through state space and emits an observation at every step.

The dishonest-casino example is a small HMM: $Y_t \in \{\text{fair}, \text{loaded}\}$ is the die in use at roll $t$, switching occasionally, and $X_t \in \{1, \ldots, 6\}$ is the observed roll. The three classical HMM questions are evaluation (how likely is the observed sequence under the model?), decoding (what was the most likely hidden state sequence?) and learning (what are the transition and emission parameters?). All three admit polynomial-time algorithms by exploiting the chain structure, and Chapter 6 takes up their derivation in detail.

3.13  Summary

A Bayesian network is a pair $(G, P)$: a directed acyclic graph and a distribution $P$ specified by a CPD at each node, with $P$ factorising as a product of "node given its parents". The graph captures asymmetric influence between variables — "causality", "generative scheme", "direction of action" — in a way the undirected world cannot. Local and global independence properties are read off by d-separation; the equivalence theorem ties the structural and factorisation specifications together. Computing the joint likelihood from the CPDs is straightforward, but marginalising over unobserved variables is in general hard, and inference must therefore be approached carefully; that is the subject of Chapter 4. Important special cases of the BN class include HMMs and tree-structured models, and many of the most successful generative models in machine learning are BNs in disguise.

It is worth flagging a small number of common misconceptions about graphical models. They do require a localist semantics for the nodes — each node is a random variable. They do not require a causal semantics for the edges — a BN can be used purely as a representational shortcut, with no commitment to a causal story. They are not necessarily Bayesian in the philosophical sense — the framework is happy with frequentist parameter estimates. And while they can be intractable in the worst case, large classes of graphs — trees, polytrees, chains, sparse high-treewidth graphs — admit efficient algorithms, and approximate inference is well-developed for the rest.
