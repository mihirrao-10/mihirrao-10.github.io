Probabilistic Graphical Models

Chapter 5: Parameter Estimation

Notes by Mihir Rao

Chapters 1–3 built the representational machinery; Chapter 4 took up inference. This chapter is the third leg of the tripod: learning — fitting parameters to data. The setup is a graphical model with a known structure and an $iid$ dataset $D = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_N\}$; the goal is the parameter vector $\theta$ that best explains $D$. Two regimes are very different. When every node is observed, the log-likelihood of a Bayesian network decomposes into a sum of local terms, and learning reduces to a series of small density-estimation problems. When some nodes are latent, the decomposition breaks down and we need the EM algorithm.

5.1  The Learning Problem

Throughout the chapter we assume the graph $G$ is fixed (from expert design or from an earlier structure-learning step), and the task is to estimate the parameters $\theta$. The principle that organises the chapter is maximum likelihood. For a fully observed BN with $iid$ data,

$$\ell(\theta; D) \;=\; \log p(D \mid \theta) \;=\; \log \prod_n \prod_i p(x_{n,i} \mid \mathbf{x}_{n,\pi_i}, \theta_i) \;=\; \sum_i \sum_n \log p(x_{n,i} \mid \mathbf{x}_{n,\pi_i}, \theta_i).$$

This is the key fact. The log-likelihood is a sum over nodes, each term involving only the local CPD parameters $\theta_i$. If we further assume parameter independence (no parameter is shared across nodes), the CPDs may be estimated independently of one another.

The natural starting point is a model with a single node.

5.2  Density Estimation as a Single-Node Graphical Model

Density estimation — fitting a distribution to $N$ samples of a single variable — is the simplest graphical model: one node, $N$ copies. Plate notation makes the structure explicit.

For the vast majority of parametric models the form chosen for $p(x \mid \theta)$ is a member of the exponential family, a class large enough to cover most of statistics and convenient enough that MLE has a closed-form structure.

Definition. Exponential family A distribution with density $$p(x \mid \eta) \;=\; h(x)\,\exp\!\bigl\{\eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta)\bigr\} \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(\eta)}\,h(x)\,\exp\!\bigl\{\eta^\top T(x)\bigr\}$$ is in the exponential family. Here $\eta$ is the natural (canonical) parameter, $T(x)$ is the sufficient statistic, $A(\eta) = \log Z(\eta)$ is the log normaliser, and $h(x)$ is the base measure. Bernoulli, multinomial, Gaussian, Poisson, gamma, and beta all fit this template.

Three properties of the exponential family carry the rest of the section.

Moment generation. Differentiating $A(\eta)$ with respect to $\eta$ pulls a $T(x)$ inside the integral:

$$\frac{\partial A(\eta)}{\partial \eta} \;=\; \int T(x)\,\frac{h(x)\,e^{\eta^\top T(x)}}{Z(\eta)}\,\mathrm{d}x \;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[T(X)\bigr] \;=\; \mu.$$

The first derivative of the log normaliser is the mean of the sufficient statistic. A second differentiation gives $\partial^2 A / \partial \eta^2 = \mathrm{Var}\bigl(T(X)\bigr) > 0$, so $A$ is convex.

Moment vs. canonical parameters. Because $A$ is strictly convex, the map $\eta \mapsto \mu(\eta) = \partial A / \partial \eta$ is invertible: a distribution in the exponential family can be parameterised equivalently by either $\eta$ or $\mu$. We will move between the two as convenient. Write $\eta = \psi(\mu)$ for the inverse.

Sufficiency. The data enters the likelihood only through $T(x)$. By the Neyman factorisation theorem, $T$ is a sufficient statistic in the classical sense: nothing in $X$ beyond $T(X)$ contains information about $\eta$, so we can compress $N$ data points to a single sufficient-statistic vector and lose nothing.

5.3  MLE for the Exponential Family is Moment Matching

The $iid$ log-likelihood under the canonical form is

$$\ell(\eta; D) \;=\; \sum_n \log h(x_n) \;+\; \eta^\top \sum_n T(x_n) \;-\; N\,A(\eta).$$

Setting the derivative with respect to $\eta$ to zero gives

$$\sum_n T(x_n) \;-\; N\,\frac{\partial A(\eta)}{\partial \eta} \;=\; 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{MLE}} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n} T(x_n).$$

The MLE in the moment parameterisation is the sample average of sufficient statistics — moment matching. The canonical parameter follows by inverting: $\hat{\eta}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \psi(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{MLE}})$. For Bernoulli the rule reduces to $\hat{p} = \bar{x}$; for Gaussian to $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$ and $\hat{\Sigma} = \overline{(x - \bar{x})(x - \bar{x})^\top}$; for multinomial to $\hat{\pi}_k = n_k / N$. The single calculation above subsumes all of them.

5.4  Generalised Linear Models

The two-node graphical model $X \to Y$ — a feature vector $X$ predicting a response $Y$ — is the next building block. The framework that unifies linear regression, logistic regression, Poisson regression, and softmax classification is the generalised linear model (GLIM).

A GLIM has three components. The linear predictor $\xi = \theta^\top x$ summarises the input. The response function $f$ maps $\xi$ to the conditional mean: $\mu = f(\xi)$. The conditional distribution $p(y \mid x)$ is an exponential family with mean $\mu$. The choice of which exponential family is constrained by the data: continuous $y$ suggests Gaussian (linear regression); a class label suggests Bernoulli (logistic regression) or multinomial (softmax). When $f$ is taken to be the inverse of the canonical map $\psi$, so that $\theta^\top x$ is the canonical parameter $\eta$, the GLIM is said to use a canonical response function: $\mu = \eta$ (Gaussian), $\mu = 1/(1 + e^{-\eta})$ (Bernoulli), $\mu_i = e^{\eta_i}/\sum_j e^{\eta_j}$ (multinomial), $\mu = e^\eta$ (Poisson).

For a canonical GLIM the gradient of the log-likelihood takes a clean form:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} \;=\; \sum_n (y_n - \mu_n)\,x_n \;=\; X^\top (\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}).$$

Setting it to zero gives the "residuals orthogonal to features" condition. Newton–Raphson with Hessian $H = -X^\top W X$ (where $W$ is the diagonal matrix of $\mathrm{d}\mu_n/\mathrm{d}\eta_n$) gives the iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) update,

$$\theta^{t+1} \;=\; (X^\top W^t X)^{-1} X^\top W^t z^t, \qquad z^t \;=\; X\theta^t + (W^t)^{-1}(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}^t),$$

so each Newton step is a weighted least-squares fit to an adjusted response. For Gaussian likelihood with identity link, $W = I$ and IRLS collapses to the normal equation $\theta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}$. For Bernoulli with logistic link, $W = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(\mu_n(1-\mu_n)\bigr)$ and we recover the standard IRLS for logistic regression.

5.5  The Likelihood Decomposes Across a Bayesian Network

The single-node and two-node pieces compose. Consider a BN on variables $X_1, \ldots, X_d$ with parameter vector $\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d)$ in which each $\theta_i$ parameterises only the CPD $p(X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i})$. The log-likelihood is

$$\ell(\theta; D) \;=\; \sum_n \sum_i \log p(x_{n,i} \mid \mathbf{x}_{n,\pi_i}, \theta_i) \;=\; \sum_i \underbrace{\sum_n \log p(x_{n,i} \mid \mathbf{x}_{n,\pi_i}, \theta_i)}_{\ell_i(\theta_i;\, D_i)}.$$

Each $\ell_i$ is a function of $\theta_i$ alone, and the data it sees is only the columns $(X_i, X_{\pi_i})$ of the dataset. Learning a BN therefore reduces to a series of small density-estimation problems — one per node, with the node and its parents as the relevant variables. Apply the single-node (§5.3) or two-node (§5.4) machinery to each.

5.6  Tabular CPDs: MLE is Counting

For discrete variables with tabular CPDs we make the per-cell parameters explicit:

$$\theta_{ijk} \;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\; p(X_i = j \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i} = k).$$

The sufficient statistics are the family-configuration counts $n_{ijk} = \#\{n : x_{n,i} = j,\,\mathbf{x}_{n, \pi_i} = k\}$, and the log-likelihood is $\sum_{i, j, k} n_{ijk}\,\log \theta_{ijk}$. Maximising subject to $\sum_j \theta_{ijk} = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier gives

$$\hat{\theta}_{ijk}^{\,\mathrm{MLE}} \;=\; \frac{n_{ijk}}{\sum_{j'} n_{ij'k}}.$$

MLE is just the empirical conditional frequency — the row of the CPT for $\mathbf{X}_{\pi_i} = k$ is the count vector of $X_i$, normalised. No iterations, no optimisation, just counting.

5.7  Latent Variables Couple Everything

The story so far depends on the dataset being completely observed. When some variables are latent, the elegant decomposition breaks down. Latent variables show up everywhere — mixture-component indicators, hidden states in speech recognition, ancestral DNA sequences, missing measurements — and the next half of the chapter is about how to learn parameters when they appear.

The two log-likelihoods carry the difference:

Definition. Complete vs. incomplete log-likelihood Let $X$ denote the observed variables and $Z$ the latent ones. The complete log-likelihood is $$\ell_c(\theta; X, Z) \;=\; \log p(X, Z \mid \theta);$$ if $Z$ were observed, $\ell_c$ would decompose as in §5.5 and MLE would proceed locally. The incomplete log-likelihood is the marginal, $$\ell(\theta; X) \;=\; \log p(X \mid \theta) \;=\; \log \sum_Z p(X, Z \mid \theta),$$ and the log of the sum couples the parameters together.

5.8  Gaussian Mixture Models

The canonical example is a Gaussian mixture model. $K$ Gaussian components $\mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ are mixed in proportions $\pi_k$:

$$p(x \mid \mu, \Sigma) \;=\; \sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k).$$

The latent-variable construction makes the structure clean. Introduce a class indicator $Z_n$ — a one-hot vector with $Z_n^k = 1$ if data point $n$ comes from component $k$. Then $p(z_n \mid \pi) = \prod_k \pi_k^{z_n^k}$ and $p(x_n \mid z_n^k = 1, \mu, \Sigma) = \mathcal{N}(x_n \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$. Marginalising $z_n$ gives the mixture density back.

If $z_n$ were observed for every $n$, the data would decompose: every $n$ goes into the bucket given by its $z_n$, and we would just fit $K$ separate Gaussians and a multinomial $\pi$. With $z_n$ unobserved, the log-likelihood

$$\ell(\theta; D) \;=\; \sum_n \log \sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_n \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$$

is a log of a sum — the $\pi_k, \mu_k, \Sigma_k$ are all entangled and direct MLE has no closed form.

5.9  Jensen's Inequality and the Lower Bound

The way around the log-of-sum is to introduce an arbitrary distribution $q(z \mid x)$ over the latents and rewrite

$$\ell(\theta; X) \;=\; \log \sum_z p(X, z \mid \theta) \;=\; \log \sum_z q(z \mid X)\,\frac{p(X, z \mid \theta)}{q(z \mid X)}.$$

Apply Jensen's inequality (concavity of the log):

$$\ell(\theta; X) \;\geq\; \sum_z q(z \mid X)\,\log \frac{p(X, z \mid \theta)}{q(z \mid X)} \;=\; \bigl\langle \ell_c(\theta; X, Z) \bigr\rangle_q \,+\, H_q,$$

where the angled brackets denote expectation under $q$ and $H_q = -\sum_z q(z \mid X) \log q(z \mid X)$ is its entropy. The right-hand side is a lower bound on the log-likelihood, valid for any choice of $q$, and it is linear in $\ell_c$ — so it inherits the decomposability the original objective lost.

Definition. Free energy $$F(q, \theta) \;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\; \sum_z q(z \mid X)\,\log \frac{p(X, z \mid \theta)}{q(z \mid X)} \;\leq\; \ell(\theta; X).$$

$F$ is a function of two arguments. The EM algorithm maximises $F$ by coordinate ascent.

5.10  EM as Coordinate Ascent on the Free Energy

Expectation–Maximisation
  1. Initialise $\theta^0$.
  2. E-step. Hold $\theta^t$ fixed; maximise $F$ over $q$: $\quad q^{t+1} \;=\; \arg\max_q F(q, \theta^t)$.
  3. M-step. Hold $q^{t+1}$ fixed; maximise $F$ over $\theta$: $\quad \theta^{t+1} \;=\; \arg\max_\theta F(q^{t+1}, \theta)$.
  4. Repeat until convergence.

5.11  The E-Step: Posterior over the Latents

Claim. $q^{t+1} = \arg\max_q F(q, \theta^t) = p(z \mid X, \theta^t)$.

The optimal $q$ at fixed $\theta^t$ is the posterior over the latents given the data and the current parameters. A direct calculation,

$$F\bigl(p(z \mid X, \theta^t),\,\theta^t\bigr) \;=\; \sum_z p(z \mid X, \theta^t)\,\log \frac{p(X, z \mid \theta^t)}{p(z \mid X, \theta^t)} \;=\; \log p(X \mid \theta^t) \;=\; \ell(\theta^t; X),$$

shows the bound is tight at this choice: $F = \ell$ when $q$ is the posterior. Equivalently, the gap $\ell - F = \mathrm{KL}\bigl(q \,\|\, p(z \mid X, \theta)\bigr)$, and KL is minimised at zero by taking $q$ to be the posterior. So the E-step is an inference problem — the very problem Chapter 4 was about.

5.12  The M-Step: Weighted MLE

Write the free energy as

$$F(q, \theta) \;=\; \bigl\langle \ell_c(\theta; X, Z) \bigr\rangle_q \,+\, H_q,$$

where the entropy $H_q$ does not depend on $\theta$. So the M-step reduces to

$$\theta^{t+1} \;=\; \arg\max_\theta\,\bigl\langle \ell_c(\theta; X, Z) \bigr\rangle_{q^{t+1}}.$$

Under the optimal $q^{t+1} = p(z \mid X, \theta^t)$ this is exactly a standard MLE on the complete log-likelihood, with the only difference that sufficient statistics involving $z$ are replaced by their expectations under $q^{t+1}$. Where MLE on fully observed data would have counted occurrences, MLE under EM counts responsibilities.

5.13  EM for Gaussian Mixtures

Specialising to a GMM with $K$ components makes the two steps explicit.

E-step. The posterior over $z_n$ given $x_n$ and current parameters is a softmax over component log-densities. Define the responsibilities

$$\tau_n^{k\,(t)} \;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\; p(z_n^k = 1 \mid x_n,\,\mu^t,\,\Sigma^t) \;=\; \frac{\pi_k^t\,\mathcal{N}(x_n \mid \mu_k^t, \Sigma_k^t)}{\sum_i \pi_i^t\,\mathcal{N}(x_n \mid \mu_i^t, \Sigma_i^t)}.$$

Each $\tau_n^{k\,(t)} \in [0, 1]$ measures how much component $k$ "owns" data point $n$ at iteration $t$.

M-step. Maximise the expected complete log-likelihood. The result is the same as the standard MLE for a Gaussian mixture treated as fully observed, with hard $z$-indicators replaced by soft responsibilities:

$$\pi_k^{t+1} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_n \tau_n^{k\,(t)}, \qquad \mu_k^{t+1} \;=\; \frac{\sum_n \tau_n^{k\,(t)}\,x_n}{\sum_n \tau_n^{k\,(t)}}, \qquad \Sigma_k^{t+1} \;=\; \frac{\sum_n \tau_n^{k\,(t)}\,(x_n - \mu_k^{t+1})(x_n - \mu_k^{t+1})^\top}{\sum_n \tau_n^{k\,(t)}}.$$

The means are weighted averages of the data; the covariances are weighted scatter matrices; the mixing weights are the average responsibilities. Each M-step update is what you would do with the corresponding fully observed data — EM has converted the latent-variable estimation problem into a sequence of weighted versions of the easy problem.

5.14  K-Means as Hard EM

K-means is the special case of GMM EM with hard responsibilities — each data point belongs entirely to its nearest cluster. The "E-step" assigns

$$z_n^{(t)} \;=\; \arg\min_k\,(x_n - \mu_k^t)^\top \Sigma_k^{-1\,(t)} (x_n - \mu_k^t),$$

and the M-step updates the centroids by the unweighted mean over each cluster's members,

$$\mu_k^{t+1} \;=\; \frac{\sum_n \delta(z_n^{(t)}, k)\,x_n}{\sum_n \delta(z_n^{(t)}, k)}.$$

It is the limit of EM as the component covariances go to zero: as the Gaussians become arbitrarily sharp the soft responsibilities $\tau_n^k$ collapse to 0/1 indicators. EM is the "soft" version of K-means and K-means is the "hard" version of EM; both are coordinate-ascent on a free-energy surrogate for the data log-likelihood.

5.15  Summary

For a fully observed Bayesian network, the log-likelihood decomposes as a sum of one term per node, and learning reduces to a series of small density-estimation problems. Single-node estimation is the exponential-family case, and MLE there is moment-matching: the canonical parameter is the value at which the model's expected sufficient statistic equals the empirical one. Two-node estimation $X \to Y$ is the generalised-linear-model case, and MLE there is iteratively reweighted least squares. For tabular CPDs, the whole thing collapses to counting.

When some variables are latent, the log-likelihood becomes a log of a sum and the decomposition breaks. The EM algorithm introduces a distribution $q$ over the latents and constructs a lower bound — the free energy — that does decompose. EM is coordinate ascent on the free energy: the E-step sets $q$ to the posterior over the latents (an inference problem), and the M-step is a weighted MLE on the complete-data model. Gaussian mixtures are the canonical example, and K-means is the hard-assignment limit of GMM EM. EM gives a local optimum, not a global one, but its template — "lower-bound the marginal log-likelihood and alternately optimise" — is the workhorse of latent-variable learning everywhere in the rest of the course.
