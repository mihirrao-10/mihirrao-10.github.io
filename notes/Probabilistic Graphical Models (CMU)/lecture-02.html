Probabilistic Graphical Models

Chapter 2: Markov Random Fields

Notes by Mihir Rao

The Bayesian networks of the previous chapter gave a compact representation of a joint distribution for problems with a natural directionality — causes preceding effects, parents preceding children. Many problems do not have one. Pixel intensities in an image, spin states in a magnet, vote intentions among friends in a social network: in each case the variables influence one another symmetrically, and imposing a directed structure is at best awkward and at worst impossible. The undirected counterpart to a Bayesian network is the Markov random field (MRF), and developing it is the subject of this chapter.

2.1  Markov Random Fields

To anchor the formalism, consider four people $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ arranged so that each pair of neighbours is friends — $A$ with $B$, $B$ with $C$, $C$ with $D$, $D$ with $A$ — and suppose each holds an upcoming-election vote $X_A, X_B, X_C, X_D \in \{0, 1\}$. We expect friends to vote alike, but there is no causal direction to the influence: $A$ does not cause $B$'s vote and $B$ does not cause $A$'s.

We score a joint configuration by an unnormalised product of factors, one per edge: $$\tilde{p}(x_A, x_B, x_C, x_D) \;=\; \phi(x_A, x_B)\,\phi(x_B, x_C)\,\phi(x_C, x_D)\,\phi(x_D, x_A),$$ where each $\phi$ encodes how compatible a friend-pair's votes are. For instance, $$\phi(x, y) \;=\; \begin{cases} 10 & x = y = 1, \\ 5 & x = y = 0, \\ 1 & x \neq y, \end{cases}$$ biases the model toward friends agreeing, with a slight extra preference for both voting $1$ over both voting $0$. The numbers are not probabilities — they are interaction weights.

To turn the unnormalised score into a probability, sum it over all $2^4 = 16$ configurations. The two unanimous outcomes give $\tilde{p}(1,1,1,1) = 10^4 = 10\,000$ and $\tilde{p}(0,0,0,0) = 5^4 = 625$, while a fully alternating outcome like $(0, 1, 0, 1)$ gives $1^4 = 1$. Summing the sixteen scores yields $Z = 11\,327$, so $$\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,1) = \tfrac{10\,000}{11\,327} \approx 0.88, \quad \mathbb{P}(0,0,0,0) = \tfrac{625}{11\,327} \approx 0.055, \quad \mathbb{P}(0,1,0,1) = \tfrac{1}{11\,327} \approx 9 \times 10^{-5}.$$ The model concentrates almost all of its probability mass on the two unanimous outcomes, with the strong bias toward $(1,1,1,1)$ that the factor weights advertise.

Definition [Markov random field]. A Markov random field is a probability distribution $p(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ over variables associated with the nodes of an undirected graph $G$, of the form $$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z}\, \prod_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \phi_c(x_c),$$ where $\mathcal{C}$ denotes the set of cliques of $G$, each $\phi_c$ is a non-negative factor on the clique $c$, and the partition function $$Z \;=\; \sum_{x_1, \ldots, x_n} \prod_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \phi_c(x_c)$$ normalises the product so that the joint sums to one.

Factors are not probability distributions. They encode the strength of interaction between mutually dependent variables — large values mean "compatible", small values mean "incompatible" — but they neither integrate to one nor have any individual probabilistic meaning. The partition function $Z$ rescales the product so the joint is a valid distribution. We are free to specify factors on only some cliques; missing factors are implicitly $\phi_c \equiv 1$.

2.2  Comparison to Bayesian Networks

The undirected formalism shines where the joint has no natural directionality, as in the voting example or in image models where adjacent pixels influence each other symmetrically. There are dependency structures that an undirected graph can express compactly that a directed graph cannot, and vice versa — the two formalisms are not nested.

Three costs come with the gain:

  • The partition function $Z$ is a sum over all joint configurations; computing it is NP-hard in general.
  • The factors are less interpretable than CPDs. Each one is just a number, not a conditional probability an expert can elicit directly.
  • There is no simple generative procedure. Unlike a Bayesian network, an MRF does not give a recipe for sampling.

A Bayesian network is itself a special case of an MRF — a particularly tractable one. Take the CPDs $p(x_i \mid x_{A_i})$ as the clique factors and the partition function $Z = 1$; the CPDs already normalise, so the product is already a valid joint. The conversion in the other direction has a name.

Definition [moralisation]. The moral graph of a directed acyclic graph $G$ is the undirected graph obtained by:
  1. adding an undirected edge between every pair of parents that share a common child (the "marrying" of co-parents); and
  2. dropping the arrowheads from every directed edge.

Once moralised, every node's parents lie in a single clique, so each CPD $p(x_i \mid x_{A_i})$ becomes a valid clique factor of the new graph. The reverse direction — building a Bayesian network for a distribution defined as an MRF — is possible but can require exponential extra structure: a small MRF may have no compact directed analogue.

2.3  Independencies in Markov Random Fields

Independence in an MRF is governed by a single rule: two variables are dependent if and only if they are connected by a path of unobserved nodes. Conversely, if every immediate neighbour of a node $X$ is observed, then $X$ is independent of every other variable in the graph.

Definition [Markov blanket]. The Markov blanket $U$ of a variable $X$ in a probability distribution $p$ is the smallest set of nodes such that $$X \;\perp\; (\mathcal{X} \setminus \{X\} \setminus U) \;\big|\; U.$$ Equivalently, $U$ is the smallest set we can condition on to make $X$ independent of every variable not in $U$ (and not equal to $X$ itself).

In an undirected model the Markov blanket has a strikingly simple description: it is exactly the set of immediate neighbours of $X$ in the graph. Once we know the values of every variable adjacent to $X$, the rest of the graph carries no further information about $X$. This is much simpler than the directed case, where the blanket also had to include the children's co-parents to seal the explaining-away path through every v-structure.

More generally, any set of observed variables that disconnects the graph creates a conditional independence between the disconnected halves. This is the undirected analogue of $d$-separation: in an MRF, ordinary graph separation does the same job, with no need for special rules around colliders.

Limitations. MRFs cannot express every kind of independence a BN can. The V-structure of a directed model — where two parents are marginally independent but become dependent once their shared child is observed — has no undirected representation. Any undirected graph either makes the parents dependent in every conditioning regime (by giving them an edge or a shared neighbour) or makes them independent regardless of observation. Explaining away is a directed-only phenomenon.

2.4  Conditional Random Fields

A conditional random field (CRF) is an MRF that models a conditional distribution $p(y \mid x)$ instead of a joint $p(x, y)$. The variables split into observed inputs $x$ and outputs $y$, and the CRF factorises the conditional over the cliques of an undirected graph on $y$, with each factor allowed to depend on the entire input.

Definition [conditional random field]. A conditional random field models a conditional distribution $$p(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \prod_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \phi_c(x_c, y_c),$$ where the input-dependent partition function is $$Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y} \prod_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \phi_c(x_c, y_c).$$

The partition function $Z(x)$ depends on the input — there is a different normalising constant for each setting of $x$.

Example [handwritten OCR]. Given a sequence of character images $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, the task is to recover the underlying letters $y_1, \ldots, y_n$. A chain CRF assigns one factor $\phi(x_i, y_i)$ per image-to-letter compatibility and one factor $\phi(y_{i-1}, y_i)$ per adjacent letter pair, capturing how often each letter pair occurs in the language (so the model prefers "th" over "tx", and so on). MAP inference picks the letter sequence that maximises $$\phi(x_1, y_1) \prod_{i=2}^{n} \phi(y_{i-1}, y_i)\, \phi(x_i, y_i).$$

In practice the factors are typically written in an exponential form: $$\phi_c(x_c, y_c) \;=\; \exp\!\bigl(\, w_c^\top f_c(x_c, y_c) \,\bigr),$$ where $f_c$ is a vector of arbitrary features of the variables in clique $c$ and $w_c$ is a learned weight vector. Features can depend on the entire input $x$ — for instance on global properties of an image — without changing the structure of the graph or the cost of inference, because they only change the values of factors, not their scopes.

Why model $p(y \mid x)$ directly. By skipping any model of $p(x)$, a CRF avoids the often-wasteful cost of modelling a high-dimensional input distribution. When all we care about is predicting $y$ from $x$, this typically yields better predictive performance with less training data than a generative model of the joint $p(x, y)$.

2.5  Factor Graphs

A factor graph is an alternative graphical representation that makes the factorisation of an MRF explicit. It is a bipartite graph: one part has a node per random variable, the other part has a node per factor, and an edge connects a factor to each of the variables in its scope.

2.6  Summary

  • A Markov random field represents a joint distribution as a normalised product of nonnegative factors over the cliques of an undirected graph: $p(x) = \tfrac{1}{Z}\prod_c \phi_c(x_c)$.
  • The partition function $Z$ is a sum over all joint configurations and is intractable in general.
  • Bayesian networks are a special case of MRFs (with $Z = 1$ and CPDs as factors). The reverse direction — moralisation — adds an undirected edge between every pair of co-parents and drops the arrowheads.
  • Independence in an MRF is governed by graph separation. The Markov blanket of a node is exactly its neighbours, and V-structures (explaining away) have no undirected analogue.
  • A conditional random field models $p(y \mid x)$ as an MRF whose factors may depend on the entire input $x$. Features in the exponential form $\phi = \exp(w^\top f)$ make them easy to parameterise.
  • A factor graph is a bipartite representation that makes the factorisation explicit and is the basis for message-passing inference.
