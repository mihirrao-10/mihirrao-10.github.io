Probabilistic Graphical Models

Lecture 2: Directed Graphical Models: Bayesian Networks

Notes by Mihir Rao

Lecture 1 sketched the promise: a probabilistic graphical model trades the exponential cost of a flat joint distribution for the manageable cost of a graph plus local potentials, and in return makes representation, learning, and inference all easier. This lecture pins down what the directed flavour of that promise actually is. We define a Bayesian network, prove the factorisation theorem that makes it work, classify the three irreducible local structures from which all directed independence reasoning is built, and develop $d$‑separation — the graph algorithm that reads off every conditional independence the network entails.

2.1  The Chain Rule Is Free; The Savings Are Not

The chain rule of probability is an identity, not an assumption. For any joint distribution over $X_1, \ldots, X_n$, $$\mathbb{P}(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{P}(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1}),$$ and the choice of variable ordering is arbitrary. This is reassuring but unhelpful on its own: each factor $\mathbb{P}(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1})$ is still a conditional distribution over an exponentially large conditioning set. We have rewritten the joint, not shrunk it.

The savings come from conditional independence assumptions. If $X_i$ is conditionally independent of its non‑parent predecessors given a small set $\mathbf{X}_{\pi_i} \subseteq \{X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1}\}$, then $$\mathbb{P}(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1}) \;=\; \mathbb{P}(X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}),$$ and each conditional shrinks from exponential in $i-1$ to exponential in $|\pi_i|$. A directed acyclic graph is the natural bookkeeping device for the family of such assumptions.

2.2  Bayesian Networks: Definition

A Bayesian network specifies a joint distribution by two pieces fitted together: a qualitative piece (which variables depend on which) and a quantitative piece (how strong each dependence is).

Definition [Bayesian network]. A Bayesian network is a pair $(G, P)$ where:
  • $G = (V, E)$ is a directed acyclic graph whose vertices index random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$;
  • $P$ is a probability distribution that factorises over $G$, in the sense that $P$ is specified by a collection of local conditional probability distributions (CPDs) $\mathbb{P}(X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i})$, one per node, where $\mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}$ is the set of parents of $X_i$ in $G$.

The graph carries the qualitative content: arrows point from cause to effect, parent to child, in‑variable to out‑variable. The CPDs carry the quantitative content. The graph alone is a commitment to a family of distributions; the CPDs pick a single member of that family.

A reading worth flagging: the same graph supports a generative procedure. Sample the root variables from their priors, then walk the topological order and sample each child from its CPD given the values its parents took. The joint distribution generated this way is, by construction, the one the network represents.

2.3  The Factorisation Theorem

Theorem (Factorisation). Let $G$ be a DAG over $X_1, \ldots, X_d$. The most general distribution consistent with $G$ — that is, the most general distribution in which every node is conditionally independent of its non‑descendants given its parents — factorises as $$\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{d} \mathbb{P}\!\left(X_i \,\middle|\, \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}\right),$$ where $\mathbf{X}_{\pi_i}$ denotes the parents of $X_i$ in $G$.

The proof has two halves, which return in §2.6 under the names soundness and completeness. The forward half — independencies imply factorisation — is a chain‑rule argument: walk the topological order, and the local Markov assumption lets each conditional in the chain rule collapse to a conditional on parents only. The reverse half — factorisation implies the local independencies — drops out from inspecting which variables appear in each factor.

Specifying a directed GM. In practice, building a Bayesian network means choosing the graph and then the CPDs. Where does the graph come from? Sometimes from prior causal or mechanistic knowledge, sometimes from expert elicitation, sometimes from a generic architecture (layered networks, chain graphs), and sometimes from data via structure learning. Where do the CPDs come from? For discrete variables they are conditional probability tables (CPTs); for continuous variables they are conditional densities — a typical example is a Gaussian whose mean is a linear function of the parent values. Either way, the local nature of the CPDs is what makes the model human‑interpretable and statistically tractable.

2.4  Three Local Structures

Every conditional independence a Bayesian network entails can be read off by looking at three irreducible patterns of three nodes and two edges. Memorise these three and you will rarely be surprised.

  • Common parent: $X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y$. The two children are marginally dependent (they share an ancestor) but become independent once their common cause $Z$ is observed. "Given the level of gene $Z$, the levels of $X$ and $Y$ are independent."
  • Cascade: $X \rightarrow Z \rightarrow Y$. Knowing $Z$ screens off everything upstream from everything downstream. "Given $Z$, $X$ provides no extra information about $Y$."
  • V‑structure: $X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow Y$. Here the polarity flips. The two parents are marginally independent — they have no common ancestor — but observing the shared child $Z$ couples them. This is explaining away: knowing one cause changes our belief about the other once an effect is in evidence.

The first two structures behave the same way: observing the middle node decouples the ends. The V‑structure is the odd one out, and it is the engine of nearly every counter‑intuitive prediction the directed formalism makes.

2.5  $d$‑separation

Generalising the three local structures to arbitrary paths in a DAG gives the $d$‑separation criterion (Pearl, 1988). A path from $X$ to $Y$ is read off as a sequence of triples; each triple is either active or inactive under a given conditioning set $\mathbf{Z}$, and the path as a whole is active only if every triple along it is.

Definition [active trail]. A trail (an undirected path with directions retained) is active under conditioning set $\mathbf{Z}$ if every consecutive triple $A - B - C$ along it is active, where:
  • Causal $A \to B \to C$: active iff $B \notin \mathbf{Z}$.
  • Evidential $A \leftarrow B \leftarrow C$: active iff $B \notin \mathbf{Z}$.
  • Common cause $A \leftarrow B \to C$: active iff $B \notin \mathbf{Z}$.
  • Common effect (V‑structure) $A \to B \leftarrow C$: active iff $B \in \mathbf{Z}$ or some descendant of $B$ is in $\mathbf{Z}$.
Definition [$d$‑separation]. Let $\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}, \mathbf{Z}$ be three disjoint sets of nodes in a DAG $G$. We say that $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ are $d$‑separated given $\mathbf{Z}$, written $\mathrm{d\text{-}sep}_G(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{Z})$, if there is no active trail between any $X \in \mathbf{X}$ and any $Y \in \mathbf{Y}$ under $\mathbf{Z}$.

An equivalent and often more efficient mental model is the Bayes‑ball algorithm: imagine a ball that wants to bounce from $X$ to $Y$, and ask whether the conditioning set $\mathbf{Z}$ permits it to. The four rules above are the bouncing rules. The set of independencies $G$ entails is $$I(G) \;=\; \bigl\{\,\mathbf{X} \perp \mathbf{Y} \,\big|\, \mathbf{Z} \;:\; \mathrm{d\text{-}sep}_G(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{Z})\,\bigr\}.$$

An equivalent geometric characterisation, useful in proofs, is: $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ are $d$‑separated given $\mathbf{Z}$ if and only if they are separated in the moralised ancestral graph of $\mathbf{X} \cup \mathbf{Y} \cup \mathbf{Z}$ — that is, the undirected graph obtained by restricting to ancestors of the relevant nodes, marrying co‑parents, and dropping arrowheads.

2.6  Soundness and Completeness

How tightly does $d$‑separation track the actual independencies of distributions that factor over $G$? Two theorems give the answer.

Theorem (Soundness of $d$‑separation). If a distribution $P$ factorises according to $G$, then every $d$‑separation independence holds in $P$: $I(G) \subseteq I(P)$.

Soundness says the graph never lies. Any independence we can extract from the graph by the Bayes‑ball rules is a genuine independence of every distribution the graph represents. This is the direction we usually rely on.

The reverse direction is more subtle. A distribution might satisfy more independencies than the graph asserts — for instance, the graph $A \to B$ permits any joint, including the joint in which $A$ and $B$ happen to be marginally independent. The completeness statement therefore has to be qualified.

Theorem (Completeness, generic). Let $G$ be a DAG and let $P$ be drawn from the family of distributions that factorise over $G$, parameterised by the CPD entries. Except on a set of CPD parameterisations of measure zero, $I(P) = I(G)$.

In words: a "generic" distribution that factors over $G$ has exactly the independencies that $G$ encodes, and the exceptions form a measure‑zero set in CPD parameter space. The non‑generic case is real but pathological — it corresponds to fine‑tuned cancellations among parameters. A useful contrapositive: if $X$ and $Y$ are not $d$‑separated given $\mathbf{Z}$ in $G$, then there exists some distribution factorising over $G$ in which $X$ and $Y$ are dependent given $\mathbf{Z}$ — not all such distributions, but some.

2.7  I-maps and I-equivalence

The relationship between a graph and the distributions it represents has a name.

Definition [I-map]. Let $P$ be a distribution and let $I(P)$ denote its set of conditional independencies. A graph $G$ is an I-map for $P$ if $I(G) \subseteq I(P)$ — every independence the graph claims actually holds in $P$.

The I-map condition is the precise statement of "$G$ does not mislead us." It is one‑directional by design: $G$ may understate the independencies that $P$ enjoys, but it must not overstate them.

Two graphs may encode exactly the same independencies even when their arrows point differently.

Definition [I-equivalence]. Two DAGs $G_1$ and $G_2$ over the same variables are I-equivalent if $I(G_1) = I(G_2)$. The relation partitions the set of DAGs into mutually exclusive I-equivalence classes.

The chain $X \to Z \to Y$, the reverse chain $X \leftarrow Z \leftarrow Y$, and the common cause $X \leftarrow Z \to Y$ all encode exactly the independence $X \perp Y \mid Z$, and so they are I-equivalent. The V‑structure $X \to Z \leftarrow Y$ is not in this class: it encodes the opposite, the marginal independence $X \perp Y$ that is destroyed by conditioning on $Z$.

Theorem (I-equivalence via skeleton + v-structures). Let $G_1$ and $G_2$ be two DAGs over the same vertex set. If $G_1$ and $G_2$ have the same skeleton (the undirected graph obtained by dropping arrowheads) and the same set of v-structures, then they are I-equivalent.

This has a striking consequence for causal inference from observational data alone: from $P$ we can recover at best the I-equivalence class of the generating graph, not the graph itself. The directions of non‑v‑structure edges are simply not identified by the independence structure of $P$.

2.8  Minimum I-maps

The complete graph — every variable an ancestor of every variable that follows it in some ordering — is an I-map for every distribution, because it asserts no independencies at all and so cannot claim a false one. This is true but useless. A graph that says nothing is not a model.

Definition [minimum I-map]. A DAG $G$ is a minimum I-map for $P$ if it is an I-map for $P$ and the removal of any single edge breaks the I-map property.

Minimum I-maps are not unique. Different variable orderings can yield different minimum I-maps for the same distribution, with different edge counts. The graph that comes from a specific ordering encodes only the independencies expressible in that ordering — the procedural minimality does not pick out a canonical structure. The lesson: when modelling, the choice of variable ordering matters, and a domain‑driven ordering (causes before effects) typically yields the most parsimonious and interpretable graph.

2.9  Plate Notation

Bayesian models with repeated, conditionally independent observations are common enough to deserve their own shorthand. Drawing $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ as $n$ separate child nodes of a parameter $\theta$ wastes ink and obscures the structure. Plate notation draws a single generic child $y_i$ inside a rectangle (a "plate") annotated with the index range, typically $i = 1{:}n$. Variables inside a plate are understood to be replicated, with the dependencies on variables outside the plate shared across all replicas.

The shorthand expresses the i.i.d. assumption visually: conditional on the parameters outside the plate, the variables inside are independent and identically distributed. Plates also nest, supporting hierarchical models in which (say) topics live in an outer plate and words within each topic live in an inner one.

2.10  A Canonical Bayesian Network: The Hidden Markov Model

The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is one of the most widely‑used Bayesian networks in practice, and a useful template for what a non‑trivial directed model looks like. The dishonest‑casino motivation: a player alternates between a fair die and a loaded die according to some hidden switching policy; we observe only the sequence of rolls. The questions one wants to answer of such a sequence — how likely is it under our model (evaluation), which rolls were fair (decoding), how loaded is the loaded die (learning) — are the canonical HMM problems.

The graphical model is a chain of hidden states $Y_1 \to Y_2 \to \cdots \to Y_T$, each emitting an observation $X_t$: $$Y_1 \to Y_2 \to \cdots \to Y_T, \qquad Y_t \to X_t \text{ for each } t.$$ By the factorisation theorem, $$p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \;=\; p(y_1) \prod_{t=2}^{T} p(y_t \mid y_{t-1}) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid y_t),$$ parameterised by a start distribution $\pi$, a state transition matrix $A$, and emission distributions per state. This is the static‑to‑dynamic generalisation of a mixture model: the components are the hidden states, and the mixture weight at time $t$ is itself a Markov chain. Algorithms for evaluation, decoding, and learning will reappear in later lectures (forward‑backward, Viterbi, Baum‑Welch).

2.11  Summary

  • A Bayesian network is a DAG plus local CPDs. The graph encodes conditional independencies; the CPDs parameterise a specific distribution.
  • The factorisation theorem identifies the family of distributions a DAG represents with $\prod_i \mathbb{P}(X_i \mid \mathbf{X}_{\pi_i})$.
  • Three local structures — common parent, cascade, V-structure — generate every conditional independence statement a DAG makes. The V-structure is the odd one out: conditioning on the collider creates dependence (explaining away).
  • $d$‑separation extends the three local rules to whole paths and is sound for every distribution that factors over the graph; it is complete for all but a measure-zero set of distributions.
  • I-equivalent graphs encode the same independencies and have the same skeleton and v-structures; pure observational data cannot distinguish within an equivalence class.
  • Plate notation compresses i.i.d. replicas, and the HMM is the canonical Bayesian network for sequence data.
