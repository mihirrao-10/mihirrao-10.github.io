Probabilistic Graphical Models

Lecture 1: Bayesian Networks

Notes by Mihir Rao

This chapter is about how to choose a probability distribution to model a real-world phenomenon. The naive joint distribution over $n$ binary variables scales as $2^n$ and is unusable past a handful of features. A Bayesian network is a compact way to parametrise a joint distribution using a directed acyclic graph. The rest of the chapter develops the chain-rule factorisation, the formal definition, the three local structures, $d$-separation, $I$-maps, and the equivalence classes they generate.

1.1  The Need for Compact Representations

Real-world modelling problems quickly run into a hard wall. Suppose we want a model for spam classification with a vocabulary of $50\,000$ words and a binary indicator $X_i$ for each word's presence in an email. The joint distribution $\mathbb{P}(X_1, \ldots, X_{50000})$ has $2^{50000}$ entries — astronomically more parameters than we could ever store, estimate, or interpret.

The goal of a graphical model is to use structure — encoded by a graph — to slash the parameter count without giving up too much modelling power. The basic idea is that most variables in a real problem are not all entangled with each other, and a compact factorisation along a directed acyclic graph is precisely the bookkeeping device that lets us write down the independencies we are willing to assume.

1.2  The Chain Rule and Factorisation

The chain rule of probability is the starting point: $$\mathbb{P}(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n) \;=\; \mathbb{P}(X_1)\,\mathbb{P}(X_2 \mid X_1)\,\cdots\,\mathbb{P}(X_n \mid X_{n-1}, \ldots, X_1).$$ This is an identity, true for any joint distribution and any ordering of the variables. On its own it provides no savings — each factor still conditions on every variable that came before it.

A Bayesian network assumes more. It assumes each variable depends only on a small set of ancestors $A_i \subseteq \{X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1}\}$ in the chosen ordering: $$\mathbb{P}(X_i \mid X_{i-1}, \ldots, X_1) \;=\; \mathbb{P}(X_i \mid X_{A_i}).$$ If each variable takes $d$ values and has at most $k$ ancestors, each conditional probability distribution (CPD) is a table of size $\mathcal{O}(d^{k+1})$, and the whole joint costs $\mathcal{O}(n\, d^{k+1})$ parameters — linear in $n$, not exponential.

The Cost of a Joint Distribution

Slide $n$ (with $d = 2$). The unstructured joint scales as $2^n$; a tree (each variable with one ancestor) as $2n - 1$; a sparse DAG with at most two ancestors as $4n - 3$.

1.3  A Worked Example: The Student Grades Model

The canonical example for this material is a small model of how a student's course outcome is generated. There are five variables:

  • $d$ — the difficulty of the course.
  • $i$ — the intelligence of the student.
  • $g$ — the student's grade in the course.
  • $s$ — the student's SAT score.
  • $l$ — the quality of the recommendation letter the student receives.

The natural generative story is: the difficulty and intelligence are determined first; the grade depends on both of them; the SAT score depends on intelligence; the recommendation letter depends on the grade.

Reading the joint distribution off the graph by conditioning each variable on its parents, $$\mathbb{P}(l, g, i, d, s) \;=\; \mathbb{P}(l \mid g)\,\mathbb{P}(g \mid i, d)\,\mathbb{P}(i)\,\mathbb{P}(d)\,\mathbb{P}(s \mid i).$$ The graph also tells us a generative story for sampling: draw $d$ and $i$ from their priors, then $g$ given $(i, d)$, then $s$ given $i$, then $l$ given $g$. Whatever values the parents took fix the conditional distributions of the children.

1.4  Formal Definition

Definition [Bayesian network]. A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph $G = (V, E)$ together with, for each node $i \in V$, a random variable $X_i$ and a conditional probability distribution $\mathbb{P}(X_i \mid X_{A_i})$, where $A_i$ denotes the parents of $i$ in $G$. The joint distribution it represents is $$\mathbb{P}(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{P}\!\left(X_i \,\middle|\, X_{A_i}\right).$$ A distribution $P$ is said to factorise over $G$ if it admits this product form.

Two consequences. The product on the right is always a valid probability distribution — non-negative and summing to one — so a Bayesian network is guaranteed to define a coherent joint. And the acyclicity requirement is not optional: a directed cycle would break the guarantee.

1.5  Three Local Structures

The next question is: which conditional independencies does a Bayesian network encode? The answer, in the small, comes from three irreducible patterns of three nodes and two edges. We write $I(p)$ for the set of conditional independencies that hold in a distribution $p$, and ask which independencies are implied by each pattern.

  • Common parent: $X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y$. Marginally, $X$ and $Y$ are dependent — they share a common cause — but conditioning on $Z$ renders them independent: $X \perp Y \mid Z$. Once we know the value of $Z$, knowing $X$ tells us nothing more about $Y$.
  • Cascade: $X \rightarrow Z \rightarrow Y$. Information from $X$ reaches $Y$ only through $Z$, so observing $Z$ blocks the channel: $X \perp Y \mid Z$ holds, but $X \not\perp Y$ marginally.
  • V-structure: $X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow Y$. Here the polarity flips. $X$ and $Y$ are marginally independent (they have no common ancestor in this small graph), but observing the shared child $Z$ couples them. This phenomenon is called explaining away.
Example [explaining away on the lawn]. Let $Z$ indicate that the lawn is wet, $X$ that it rained, $Y$ that the sprinkler ran. Marginally rain and sprinkler are independent. But if we observe a wet lawn ($Z$ true) and learn that the sprinkler did not run, the rain becomes much more likely — the two causes are now coupled by the shared effect.

1.6  $d$-Separation

The three local structures generalise to arbitrary paths in a DAG through the $d$-separation criterion. By convention, a "path" here means an undirected walk with the original edge directions retained. A path is read off as a sequence of triples; the whole path is active under a conditioning set $O$ only if every triple along it is active.

Definition [active path]. An undirected path is active given an observed set $O$ if every consecutive triple $X - Y - Z$ along it satisfies one of:
  • $X \leftarrow Y \leftarrow Z$ and $Y \notin O$;
  • $X \rightarrow Y \rightarrow Z$ and $Y \notin O$;
  • $X \leftarrow Y \rightarrow Z$ and $Y \notin O$;
  • $X \rightarrow Y \leftarrow Z$ and $Y$ or one of its descendants is in $O$.
Definition [$d$-separation]. Two disjoint sets of variables $Q$ and $W$ are $d$-separated given an observed set $O$ if there is no active path between any node in $Q$ and any node in $W$ under $O$.

The key theorem connecting graph structure and probability:

Theorem. If a distribution $p$ factorises over $G$, then every $d$-separation independence in $G$ holds in $p$: $I(G) \subseteq I(p)$. In this case $G$ is said to be an $I$-map for $p$.

The converse does not generally hold: $p$ may satisfy additional independencies the graph does not assert. The graph never claims a false independence, but it can understate the true ones.

1.7  Representational Power and Imperfect Maps

Every distribution admits at least one Bayesian network as an $I$-map — the fully connected graph asserts no independencies and is therefore an $I$-map for any joint. But this is trivial: a useful graph is one that captures real independencies, ideally without overclaiming.

A natural question is whether we can always find a graph whose independencies exactly match those of the distribution. The answer is no.

Definition [perfect map]. A graph $G$ is a perfect map for a distribution $p$ if $I(G) = I(p)$.

Perfect maps do not always exist.

Example [noisy XOR]. Let $X, Y$ be independent Bernoulli$(1/2)$ variables, and define $Z = X \oplus Y$. The pairwise marginals are all uniform and independent: $X \perp Y$, $X \perp Z$, and $Y \perp Z$. But $X$, $Y$, $Z$ together are not mutually independent — any one of them is determined by the other two. No three-node Bayesian network can encode the three pairwise independencies without also implying mutual independence, so no DAG on these three variables is a perfect map for this distribution.

1.8  $I$-Equivalence

Even when perfect maps do exist, they are not unique. Distinct DAGs can encode exactly the same set of conditional independencies.

Definition [$I$-equivalence]. Two DAGs $G_1$ and $G_2$ over the same variables are $I$-equivalent if $I(G_1) = I(G_2)$. The relation partitions all DAGs on a fixed vertex set into equivalence classes.

The chain $X \to Y \to Z$ and the reverse chain $X \leftarrow Y \leftarrow Z$ both encode the single independence $X \perp Z \mid Y$, and are therefore $I$-equivalent. The two-node graphs $X \to Y$ and $X \leftarrow Y$ assert no independencies and are also $I$-equivalent. A syntactic test isolates the structure responsible.

Definition [skeleton]. The skeleton of a DAG $G$ is the undirected graph obtained by dropping the arrowheads — an edge $\{X, Y\}$ for every directed edge $(X, Y)$ in $G$.
Theorem. If $G_1$ and $G_2$ have the same skeleton and the same set of v-structures, then $I(G_1) = I(G_2)$.

Reversing an edge that is not part of a v-structure leaves the encoded independencies unchanged; reversing an edge that participates in a v-structure breaks $I$-equivalence.

1.9  Summary

  • A naive joint over $n$ binary variables has $2^n$ entries. A Bayesian network trades this for a product of small local conditionals, one per node given its parents in a DAG.
  • A Bayesian network always defines a valid joint distribution; cyclic graphs do not.
  • Three local structures — common parent, cascade, V-structure — generate every conditional independence claim a DAG can make. The V-structure is the odd one out (explaining away).
  • $d$-separation extends the three local rules to arbitrary paths. If $p$ factorises over $G$, then $I(G) \subseteq I(p)$ — the graph is an $I$-map for $p$.
  • Perfect maps do not always exist; the noisy-XOR distribution is the standard counterexample.
  • Two graphs are $I$-equivalent iff they have the same skeleton and the same v-structures.
