Probabilistic Graphical Models

Chapter 1: Introduction to GM

Notes by Mihir Rao

This first chapter motivates why graphical models exist at all. Joint distributions over many variables are unmanageable in their raw form, and a graph turns out to be the right scaffold for both representing structure and computing with it. Before we get to specific model families, we need a vocabulary for the kinds of relationships a graph might encode — correlation, dependence, conditional dependence, and their measurements. We will move from the simplest measure (Pearson's correlation) through stronger marginal measures (mutual information, HSIC) and on to the conditional analogues (partial correlation, conditional independence), and see how each gives rise to a different kind of graph.

1.1  Why Do We Need Graphical Models?

Suppose we want to model the joint distribution of $d$ random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_d$. If each $X_i$ takes $k$ possible values, the joint table $\mathbb{P}(X_1, \ldots, X_d)$ has $k^d$ entries. Even for $d = 8$ binary variables that is $2^8 = 256$ parameters; for the $d \approx 20\,000$ genes in a cell it is hopeless. We can neither store the table, estimate it from data, nor reason about it.

The way out is to impose structure. Most variables in a real system are not all entangled with each other — they interact through a smaller set of pathways. If we can describe these pathways with a graph, the joint factorises into a product of much smaller terms, and the parameter count drops from exponential to roughly linear in $d$.

A canonical example is a cellular signal transduction network. Two membrane receptors $X_1, X_2$ pick up extracellular signals and activate downstream kinases $X_3, X_4, X_5$, which in turn control a transcription factor $X_6$, which then regulates two genes $X_7, X_8$. For binary variables this factorisation costs $1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 4 = 18$ free parameters instead of $2^8 - 1 = 255$ — an order-of-magnitude reduction, and the savings grow with $d$. The graph is doing real work: it tells us which conditional probabilities we need to specify and, equivalently, which independence assumptions we are willing to make.

1.2  What Is a Graphical Model?

Informally, a graphical model is a graph whose nodes are random variables and whose edges encode some kind of relationship between them. The whole subject is about making "some kind of relationship" precise — pinning down what the edges mean, what they let us compute, and how they constrain the joint distribution.

Between any two random variables $X$ and $Y$ there is a surprisingly long list of possible relationships, even before we bring a third variable into the picture:

  • $X$ and $Y$ are correlated — the simplest linear notion of "moving together".
  • $X$ and $Y$ are dependent — a stronger statement, $\mathbb{P}(X, Y) \neq \mathbb{P}(X)\mathbb{P}(Y)$, allowing arbitrary nonlinear coupling.
  • $X$ and $Y$ are independent: $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$.
  • $X$ and $Y$ are partially correlated given $Z$ — correlated after the linear effect of $Z$ has been removed.
  • $X$ and $Y$ are conditionally (in)dependent given $Z$: $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$.
  • $X$ causes $Y$, $Y$ causes $X$, both, or a common cause $Z$ causes both.

A graphical model picks one of these notions, fixes a meaning for "edge between $X$ and $Y$", and asks: what graph best describes a given joint distribution? The flavour of the model — marginal dependency graph, Markov random field, Bayesian network, structural causal model — comes from this choice.

1.3  Pearson's Correlation: the Linear Marginal Measure

The most familiar measure of association is Pearson's correlation between two scalar random variables:

Definition. Pearson's correlation $$\rho(X, Y) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)\,\mathrm{Var}(Y)}} \;\in\; [-1, 1].$$

$\rho$ captures the linear part of the relationship. If $Y$ is exactly a linear function of $X$, $|\rho| = 1$; if $Y$ depends on $X$ in a nonlinear-but-symmetric way (e.g. $Y = X^2$ with $X$ centred at zero), $\rho$ can be zero even though $X$ and $Y$ are very much dependent. This is the core weakness of Pearson's correlation as a notion of association:

  • $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \;\Longrightarrow\; \rho(X, Y) = 0.$
  • $\rho(X, Y) = 0 \;\not\Longrightarrow\; X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y.$

So Pearson's correlation is a weak measure: it detects linear association but misses anything else. We need stronger marginal measures that fire whenever $X$ and $Y$ are dependent at all.

1.4  Stronger Marginal Measures: Distance Between Joint and Product

$X$ and $Y$ are independent iff $\mathbb{P}_{X,Y} = \mathbb{P}_X \mathbb{P}_Y$. The most direct way to measure dependence, then, is to pick a distance (or divergence) on distributions and compute

$$\text{dependence}(X, Y) \;=\; \mathrm{dist}\!\bigl(\mathbb{P}_{X,Y}, \;\mathbb{P}_X \mathbb{P}_Y\bigr).$$

Two choices of "dist" lead to the two strong measures that show up everywhere in the rest of the course.

1.5  Mutual Information

Using the Kullback–Leibler divergence gives the mutual information:

Definition. Mutual information $$I(X, Y) \;=\; \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\mathbb{P}_{X,Y} \,\|\, \mathbb{P}_X \mathbb{P}_Y\bigr) \;=\; \iint \mathbb{P}(x, y) \log \frac{\mathbb{P}(x, y)}{\mathbb{P}(x)\,\mathbb{P}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.$$

Mutual information has the property that Pearson's correlation lacks:

Fact. $I(X, Y) \geq 0$, and $I(X, Y) = 0$ if and only if $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$.

So $I(X, Y)$ is a true measure of dependence: it is zero exactly when the variables are independent, regardless of whether the dependence is linear, quadratic, or otherwise. The price is that estimating $I$ from samples is much harder than estimating $\rho$ — we need a good estimate of the joint density (or a clever variational lower bound).

1.6  Hilbert–Schmidt Independence Criterion

The KL divergence is one way to measure distance between distributions, but it requires densities. A more recent alternative uses maximum mean discrepancy (MMD): embed each distribution into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) by computing the mean of feature maps, and measure the squared distance between the two embeddings.

Definition. HSIC — for kernels $k$ on $\mathcal{X}$ and $\ell$ on $\mathcal{Y}$, $$\mathrm{HSIC}(X, Y) \;=\; \mathrm{MMD}^2\bigl(\mathbb{P}_{X,Y}, \;\mathbb{P}_X \mathbb{P}_Y\bigr),$$ computed in the product RKHS with kernel $k \otimes \ell$.

For characteristic kernels (e.g. the Gaussian RBF), $\mathrm{HSIC}(X, Y) = 0$ if and only if $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$ — the same strong property as mutual information. HSIC has the advantage that it can be estimated from samples without ever estimating a density: only kernel evaluations between data points are needed.

1.7  Marginal Dependency Graphs and Their Limits

Equipped with a marginal measure of association — Pearson's $\rho$, mutual information $I$, or HSIC — we can already build a graphical model: for each pair of variables $(X_i, X_j)$, draw an edge if the chosen measure is nontrivially nonzero. The result is a marginal dependency graph, the most primitive form of graphical model.

This is rarely informative enough to be useful. Consider three variables for a population of children:

  • $H$ = height,
  • $V$ = vocabulary size,
  • $A$ = age.

All three are marginally correlated — older kids are taller and know more words. The marginal dependency graph is the complete triangle on $\{H, V, A\}$, which suggests, misleadingly, that height and vocabulary are directly related. They are not: once you know the child's age, height tells you nothing further about vocabulary. The "right" graph removes the $H \!-\! V$ edge:

1.8  Partial Correlation

The partial correlation of $X$ and $Y$ given a random vector $\mathbf{Z}$ is the correlation between $X$ and $Y$ after the linear effect of $\mathbf{Z}$ has been regressed out of each. Concretely, let

$$e_X \;=\; X - (\boldsymbol{\beta}_X^\top \mathbf{Z} + \text{intercept}_X), \qquad e_Y \;=\; Y - (\boldsymbol{\beta}_Y^\top \mathbf{Z} + \text{intercept}_Y)$$

be the residuals from least-squares regressions of $X$ and $Y$ on $\mathbf{Z}$. Then

Definition. Partial correlation $$\rho(X, Y \mid \mathbf{Z}) \;=\; \rho(e_X, e_Y) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(e_X, e_Y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(e_X)\,\mathrm{Var}(e_Y)}}.$$

Like Pearson's correlation, partial correlation is one-sided as a test of independence:

  • $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid \mathbf{Z} \;\Longrightarrow\; \rho(X, Y \mid \mathbf{Z}) = 0.$
  • $\rho(X, Y \mid \mathbf{Z}) = 0 \;\not\Longrightarrow\; X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid \mathbf{Z}.$

For a $d$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \ldots, X_d)$, the partial correlation graph has an edge $(i, j)$ whenever $\rho(X_i, X_j \mid \mathbf{X}_{-ij}) \neq 0$, where $\mathbf{X}_{-ij}$ denotes all variables except $X_i$ and $X_j$. The entries

$$R_{ij} \;=\; \rho(X_i, X_j \mid \mathbf{X}_{-ij})$$

look daunting — naively we would need to run $\binom{d}{2}$ multivariate regressions. But there is a beautiful shortcut. Let $\Sigma$ be the covariance matrix of $\mathbf{X}$ and $\Theta = \Sigma^{-1}$ its inverse (the precision matrix). Then

Fact. $\displaystyle R_{ij} \;=\; -\,\frac{\Theta_{ij}}{\sqrt{\Theta_{ii}\,\Theta_{jj}}}.$

So the partial correlation graph is read straight off the sparsity pattern of the precision matrix: $R_{ij} = 0$ iff $\Theta_{ij} = 0$. This identity is the reason precision matrices are the central object in undirected graphical models for Gaussian data.

1.9  Conditional Independence and the Gaussian Shortcut

The general notion we actually want is full conditional independence: $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid \mathbf{Z}$, no linearity assumption. The bad news is that measuring conditional independence in full generality is hard — harder than measuring partial correlation, harder than mutual information, harder than HSIC. Conditional density estimation is notoriously difficult in high dimensions, and turning that into a reliable test is an active research area.

The good news is that under a Gaussian assumption everything collapses to partial correlation:

Fact. If $(X, Y, \mathbf{Z})$ are jointly Gaussian, then $\rho(X, Y \mid \mathbf{Z}) = 0$ if and only if $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid \mathbf{Z}$.

Under joint Gaussianity, partial correlation is both necessary and sufficient for conditional independence — and so the precision-matrix shortcut from the previous section reads off the full conditional independence structure. Many results in the graphical-models literature implicitly rely on this Gaussian assumption, and it is worth being aware of when it is being made.

1.10  A Map of Association Measures

The measures we have seen split along two axes: marginal vs. conditional, and linear vs. nonlinear. Each cell points to a different way of building a graphical model.

1.11  What's Next

From here the course follows the structure suggested by the right-hand branch of the tree above. The conditional independence graph — under various names (Markov network, Markov random field, undirected graphical model) — is the subject of the next chapter. Then we turn to directed acyclic graphs, where the cellular-signalling example from the start of this chapter becomes the canonical Bayesian network. With those two model classes in hand we can ask, given a graphical model:

  • Inference. What is the marginal or conditional density of a variable? Its mean? Its mode? Can we sample from it?
  • Learning. Given data, how do we estimate the parameters of a model with a fixed structure, and how do we choose the structure?

The recurring slogan is M. Jordan's: probability theory provides the glue that combines the parts, while graph theory provides both an interface for human modelling and a data structure for efficient algorithms. Many of the workhorse multivariate models from statistics, information theory, and pattern recognition — mixture models, hidden Markov models, Kalman filters, Boltzmann machines — turn out to be special cases of this common formalism. The point of the rest of the course is to make that statement precise.
