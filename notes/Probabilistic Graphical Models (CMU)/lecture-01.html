Probabilistic Graphical Models

Lecture 1: Introduction to Graphical Models

Notes by Mihir Rao

A probabilistic graphical model (PGM) is a marriage of probability and graph theory: a graph $G = (V, E)$ whose vertices index random variables and whose edges encode probabilistic relationships among them. The graph is not decoration. It is the data structure that makes representation compact, learning sample‑efficient, and inference algorithmically feasible for joint distributions over many variables. Michael Jordan put it cleanly: probability theory is the glue that combines the parts, graph theory is the interface by which humans specify highly‑interacting sets of variables, and the resulting structure lends itself to general‑purpose algorithms. Most classical multivariate models — in statistics, systems engineering, information theory, pattern recognition, statistical mechanics — turn out to be special cases of this single formalism.

The field is older than its name. Sewall Wright's path analysis dates to the 1920s; the modern theory was developed independently in the late 1980s by Spiegelhalter and Lauritzen in statistics and Pearl in computer science. The slogan that has survived is $$\textsc{GM} \;=\; \text{Multivariate Statistics} \,+\, \text{Structure}.$$

1.1  The Three Fundamental Questions

Every probabilistic modelling exercise turns on three intertwined questions.

  • Representation. How do we capture uncertainty in possible worlds, and how do we encode domain knowledge, assumptions, and constraints?
  • Inference. Given a model and (possibly partial) data, how do we answer queries such as $\mathbb{P}(X_i \mid \mathcal{D})$?
  • Learning. Among a family $\mathfrak{M}$ of candidate models, which is right for the data? $$\mathcal{M}^\star \;=\; \arg\max_{\mathcal{M} \in \mathfrak{M}}\, F(\mathcal{D};\, \mathcal{M}).$$

Each becomes hard the moment the number of variables grows beyond a handful, and each is what graphical structure is designed to tame.

1.2  Why the Joint Distribution Is the Wrong Starting Point

To see why structure is needed, consider the most general specification of a joint distribution over $n$ binary random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$, $$\mathbb{P}(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n).$$ Three things go wrong all at once.

  • Representation. Specifying the joint requires a table of $2^n$ entries; storage is exponential in $n$.
  • Learning. Estimating $2^n$ probabilities by maximum likelihood requires a sample size that scales exponentially with $n$ — and we have only finite data.
  • Inference. Computing the conditional $p(H \mid A)$ of unobserved variables $H$ given evidence $A$ means marginalising the joint over the remaining unobserved configurations, exponential in their count.

Equally telling: even if we could write the table down we would gain no scientific insight from it. Domain experts do not think in $2^n$ flat probability values; they think in mechanisms.

What graphical structure buys. The cure is conditional independence. If the variables satisfy enough conditional independencies, the joint factors into a product of much smaller, locally interpretable terms, and all three questions — representation, inference, learning — shrink accordingly. The graph is the bookkeeping device that records which independencies we assume.

1.4  Bayesian Learning, with Structure as a Prior

Once the graph has supplied a tractable parameterisation, learning proceeds by Bayes' rule. Letting $\theta$ denote model parameters, $\mathbf{A}$ the observed data, and $\chi$ a hyperparameter, $$\underbrace{p(\theta \mid \mathbf{A},\, \chi)}_{\text{posterior}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(\mathbf{A} \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\, \underbrace{p(\theta;\, \chi)}_{\text{prior}},$$ and the Bayes estimate of a query of $\theta$ is the posterior expectation $$\theta_{\text{Bayes}} \;=\; \int \theta\, p(\theta \mid \mathbf{A},\, \chi)\, d\theta.$$ The graphical structure is what makes both pieces tractable: the likelihood factors along the graph, and the marginalisation decomposes into operations on small local potentials. In the Bayesian slogan, knowledge meets data, and the graph is the interface across which the meeting happens.

1.5  Two Families: Bayesian Networks and Markov Random Fields

There are two basic flavours of graphical model, distinguished by whether the edges are directed.

Definition [Bayesian network]. A Bayesian network (BN), or directed graphical model, is a directed acyclic graph $G = (V, E)$ together with a collection of local conditional probability distributions (CPDs) $\mathbb{P}(X_v \mid X_{\mathrm{pa}(v)})$, one per node $v$. The joint distribution is the product $$\mathbb{P}(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \;=\; \prod_{v \in V} \mathbb{P}\!\left(X_v \,\middle|\, X_{\mathrm{pa}(v)}\right).$$ Directed edges are typically read as causality, or, more cautiously, as generative dependence.

A node in a BN is conditionally independent of every other node given its Markov blanket — the union of its parents, its children, and the other parents of its children (its children's co‑parents). The CPDs and the DAG together determine the joint, and the graph supports a generative procedure: draw the root variables from their priors, then each child from its CPD given its parents.

Definition [Markov random field]. A Markov random field (MRF), or undirected graphical model, is an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ together with nonnegative potential functions $\psi_C$ on the cliques $C$ of $G$. The joint distribution is the normalised product $$\mathbb{P}(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z}\prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}(G)} \psi_C\!\left(X_C\right),\qquad Z = \sum_x \prod_C \psi_C(x_C).$$ Edges encode correlations: a node is conditionally independent of the rest of the graph given its immediate neighbours.

The two families serve different intuitions. Bayesian networks are natural where there is plausible causal or generative structure — genetics, diagnosis, signal cascades. Markov random fields are natural where the only honest relation is undirected adjacency — pixels in an image, atoms in a crystal, words in a document. A standard caveat: MRFs encode correlations but provide no explicit sampling procedure, since the normaliser $Z$ couples the entire graph.

Example [two factorisations of the signalling network]. Reading the signalling DAG as a Bayesian network gives a product of CPDs (toggle "Bayesian Network" above). Turning every directed edge undirected and adding moralisation edges between co‑parents gives the Markov random field with energy form $$\mathbb{P}(X_1, \ldots, X_8) = \tfrac{1}{Z}\,\exp\bigl\{E(X_1) + E(X_2) + E(X_3,X_1) + E(X_4,X_2) + E(X_5,X_2) + E(X_6,X_3,X_4) + E(X_7,X_6) + E(X_8,X_5,X_6)\bigr\}.$$ The same graph topology yields two different parameterisations.

1.6  Separation and the Equivalence Theorem

The whole point of drawing a graph is that the graph should imply the right independencies: any independence one can read off the graph by a separation criterion should hold in any distribution the graph represents, and conversely, any distribution that factors along the graph should obey those independencies.

Theorem (Equivalence, informal). Fix a graph $G$, and write $I(G)$ for the set of conditional independencies entailed by $G$ via its separation criterion. Let $\mathcal{D}_1$ be the family of all distributions that satisfy $I(G)$, and let $\mathcal{D}_2$ be the family of all distributions that factor according to $G$. Then $\mathcal{D}_1 \equiv \mathcal{D}_2$.

The precise separation criterion differs for directed and undirected graphs — $d$‑separation in the directed case, ordinary graph separation in the undirected case — and we will state both carefully in the next two lectures. What matters now is the role of the theorem: it makes the graph into a language. Drawing a graph is the same as committing to a parameterised family of distributions, and writing down a factorised distribution is the same as committing to the graph one is implicitly using. Either direction supplies all of the other.

1.7  Graphical Models Are Your Old Friends

A surprising amount of classical machine learning and statistics is graphical‑model territory in disguise.

  • Density estimation — a single node $X$ with an optional parameter parent carrying $(\mu, \sigma)$.
  • Regression — a two‑node graph $X \to Y$, linear or otherwise.
  • Classification — a two‑node graph $Q \to X$ (generative) or $Q \leftarrow X$ (discriminative).
  • Clustering — a latent label generating observations, $Q \to X$ with $Q$ unobserved.

A wider zoo — hidden Markov models, Kalman filters and other linear dynamical systems, factor analysis, ICA, mixtures of factor analysers, factorial HMMs, switching state‑space models, Boltzmann machines — forms a connected genealogy in which graphical structure is the family resemblance. Beyond classical learning, fancier graphical models reach into reinforcement learning (partially observable MDPs as a temporal DAG over states, actions, observations, and rewards), machine translation (latent topic models that align word streams across languages), genetic pedigrees (allele networks across generations), and solid‑state physics (Ising and Potts models on a lattice). The value proposition is the same across the board: a language for communication, a language for computation, and a language for development.

1.8  A Few Myths

  • GMs require a localist semantics for nodes. True — and this is what makes the graph informative.
  • GMs require a causal semantics for edges. False. Directed edges may be causal but need not be; undirected edges are just correlations.
  • GMs are necessarily Bayesian. False. The framework is equally hospitable to maximum‑likelihood estimation.
  • GMs are intractable. Mostly false. Exact inference is intractable in general, but tractable subclasses (trees, low‑treewidth graphs) cover a great deal, and approximate algorithms (variational methods, Monte Carlo) cover much of the rest.
